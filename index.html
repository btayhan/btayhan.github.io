      Over time, my interests in molecular biology became increasingly focused on computational biophysics. I was especially fascinated by the possibility of looking at proteins not as single static structures, but as molecules that move between many conformations, and asking what controls those movements.

      A large part of my early work focused on calmodulin (CaM). CaM is extremely flexible, which is also what makes it a difficult system to describe computationally. During my M.Sc., I used molecular dynamics and well-tempered metadynamics to explore how its conformational landscape changes with calcium binding and ionic strength. Rather than asking which single structure is the “correct” one, I became interested in how the environment changes the relative accessibility of different states and how those states relate to the conformations CaM uses when interacting with its targets. This work eventually became my first first-author paper.

      At the same time, I started working on a very different scale of problem with dihydrofolate reductase (DHFR). Instead of following the conformational landscape of one protein under a few environmental conditions, here I am interested in what happens when the sequence itself is systematically changed. Using alchemical free-energy calculations, I have been studying how mutations alter the relative binding of different DHFR inhibitors and how these molecular energetic changes relate to experimentally measured fitness. Doing this for hundreds of mutations also pushed me into high-performance computing, automation, and building workflows that can handle thousands of simulations rather than treating each calculation as an isolated case.

      These projects have shaped the direction of my Ph.D. I am increasingly interested in the space between detailed molecular simulations and large-scale mutational data: how much molecular detail do we actually need to understand the effect of a mutation, and how can we extract that information without performing an expensive simulation for every possible variant? My current work therefore combines molecular simulation, free-energy calculations, protein structural analysis, machine learning, and HPC, depending on what the biological question requires.

      What I enjoy most is moving between these different scales, from individual molecular interactions to conformational landscapes and eventually to thousands of protein variants, and trying to connect the computational results back to something biologically interpretable.
